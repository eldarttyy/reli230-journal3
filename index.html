Prompt #8 · Where is your heart at home?

The kid who came home late and dirty

When I hear the word home, the first thing that comes to my mind is the house I grew up in, right between Issyk-Kul lake and the northern Tian Shan mountains. It had hot food and warmth every day of my life. The cats playing around and the local birds singing in the garden remind me that I am home. I could smell the delicious food my mom or dad made every day at 9pm, right by the gate. My parents and sisters were there, waiting for the kid who came from soccer practice very late and dirty.

Our textbook has a word for this kind of place. Kith means intimate knowledge of a deeply familiar landscape, a landscape filled with relatives (Crawford O'Brien 142). I know that house by its birds, its cats and the smell at the gate, so it is my kith. Kimmerer imagines the natural world telling us that it has “sung you awake in the morning” (qtd. in Crawford O'Brien 149), and I think of the birds in our garden.

I remember the summer days when all the fruit in the garden was ripe and the smell of the apricots attracted bees. That is also where I have my allergy. With the fruit came the duty to collect it and make homemade juice from apricots and apples, and kompot, a sweet drink made by simmering fruit in water with sugar. I still remember collecting with my whole family, everybody healthy, happy and working together on the juice we would enjoy all year. My mother told me the most important step is to steam the glass bottles, so the juice can be stored for years without mold or any chemicals. Salmón calls it kincentric ecology when people see nature as an extended family (148). In our garden the two families worked together. We took care of the trees, and the trees fed us the whole year.

The garden

The highest part of the tree

The first apricots

While you collect the apricots, you try the nicest out of hundreds, and you enjoy it at the highest part of the tree, where they ripen first and turn reddish from the sun. My sisters were always by my side, handing me empty buckets and hoping I would not fall. In Nuu-chah-nulth teaching, asking for help when you need it affirms the unity of creation (148). I could not collect from the top without them.

The warm roof

The tree grew so big that it was easy to collect from the roof of our storage. The roof was so warm that I would put my cap under my head and sleep under blue skies. I could see the whole neighborhood and kids playing soccer, so I knew when to go join them. From up there I could see that everything was connected: the tree, the roof, my family and my friends. The Nuu-chah-nulth phrase hishuk ish tsawalk says it as “everything is alive, and everything is connected” (147).

Green, with salt

My father taught me how to plant trees and care for them through the seasons. You wait with patience for the day you can enjoy the fruit. Sometimes you try it while it is still green, with salt, which means the day is closer than ever. I used to bring a box to my aunt, who loves our apricots. The care my father put into a tree reached her too, so for me kinship passes from the land to the family and then to others.

Prompt #8 · The animals

They saw bulls. I saw friends.

We also had animals at home, around 20 to 30 sheep and some bulls, and I used to take care of them. It taught me responsibility. If I forgot to feed them, they would be hungry the whole day. There were times when I did not make it, or I thought I had fed them but I had not, and it made me feel so bad. The textbook describes the Islamic teaching of stewardship, that people are “personally responsible for the care and preservation of their brethren communities,” human, plant or animal (158). I was learning it from hungry sheep. They depended on me, and forgetting had a cost for them.

The sheep would come up to me, not afraid, and the big bulls recognized me as their friend. Once, when we had to sell them, three or four big guys came to see the bulls. They saw me feeding bulls that were ten times bigger than me, and I was not afraid, because we were friends. They were shocked. Nuu-chah-nulth philosophy respects the distinct personhood of all living beings (147), and that helps me explain what those men saw. The bulls knew who I was, and I knew them. The Rarámuri idea of Iwí, which Salmón describes, is that all of life shares one breath and is in constant kinship (148). I think the sheep and bulls were part of my family in that way.

I feel like I have roots leading to that place all the time. Part of me misses those days and wants to go home, see it with my own eyes and look for the feelings I had as a kid. Kimmerer writes about species loneliness, how we miss our relatives in nature and they miss us too (149). I am far from my parents and sisters, but I am also far from the trees and the animals, so I understand this loneliness. When life is hard, I think about those days and how far I have come, far from home and from my family. That makes me value that place even more. The main idea of this chapter is that resilience comes from relationships, and those memories are how home still gives me strength.

Prompt #10 · Appropriation and learning

Playing the komuz for visitors

When someone uses a culture that isn't theirs, I am often happy and excited. I believe it can be the deepest way to express interest and engagement. During the Nomad Games in my hometown, there are athletes from more than 100 countries. Seeing them celebrate our nomad culture is fun, and I get the opportunity to share it with them. The reading helped me see why this feels good. The difference between appropriation and exchange is power: appropriation reinforces inequality, while exchange done right advances justice (198). At the Games I was sharing my own culture, with guests who came to learn about it.

I played the komuz, a traditional instrument, for visitors, and their interest and excitement made it fun for me too. Those are the moments I want to live. Thich Nhat Hanh and Sister True Devotion teach compassionate listening, where you listen in order to understand, and it leads to authentic connection (182). The visitors' interest in the komuz reminds me of that. Coming to America to learn about its culture and people was also one of my biggest dreams, and experiencing it was very fun. I was now the guest, learning somebody else's culture.

But using a culture can also cause harm. Bucar says appropriation “relies on, and contributes to existing forms of structural injustice” (qtd. in Crawford O'Brien 193). Smudging is a meaningful part of ceremony for many Native people, but it has become common among outsiders, and the demand for white sage has led to overharvesting (196). That is different from guests being invited to the Games or listening to a song. The chapter asks us to learn the history, go where we have permission and look for ways to give back (195). For me, that is the line between copying and learning.

Prompt #11 · Envision hope

Something you choose and do

I think hope is a strong belief in something. It could be anything, but it is so strong that it helps people overcome challenges.

Now I think of hope as something you choose and do, a decision affected by a person's culture and experience. Crawford O'Brien calls hope a ceremony, a practice, and compares it to a seed that we plant (217). That takes me back to my father teaching me to plant trees. We cared for them through the seasons and waited with patience for the fruit.

I believe my mom is the most hopeful person, and in a way it brings good luck. When you hope and take action, I believe it will happen for sure. Stoknes would call this Active Hope, the belief that if I do my part, everything will be fine (218). Tutu challenges me here. He says hope is a sailor in a storm who keeps rowing no matter what (218). The sailor does not know the ending. We both agree that hope is something you do.

With climate change, Macy and Johnstone say we are choosing between three stories: Business as Usual, the Great Unraveling and the Great Turning (223). If hope is a choice, then choosing the Great Turning is part of it. One person's action can look small, but adrienne maree brown's boiling kettle shows how small bubbles add up to a full boil (225). Feeding the animals every day was also a small action, and they depended on it.
